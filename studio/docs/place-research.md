# Placeform — Central Eastside evidence brief

Research checked 8 September 2026. Speculative data-center design study near SE Water Avenue / SE Main Street, Portland. Nine core official sources below; historical reports are identified by year and are not current parcel due diligence.

## Site status and framing

Use this visible label: **“Speculative study near SE Water Avenue and SE Main Street. Site boundary is illustrative; parcel availability, ownership, zoning approval, utilities, and engineering feasibility have not been established.”**

Do not describe the selected footprint as vacant, available, owned, approved, or a real development proposal. A nearby intersection gives geographic context; it does not establish a legal parcel. Do not imply that a real occupied warehouse will be demolished.

Working design thesis (interpretation): **A compact industrial building with a useful civic edge: warehouse-scale bays, tactile masonry, a sheltered pedestrian frontage, native rain planting, and a mechanical core organized for acoustic control.**

## Nine source cards: evidence → design response

### S1 — Industrial history

[City of Portland — Portland’s Central Eastside, 2013](https://www.portland.gov/sites/default/files/2020-01/portlands-central-eastside-2013.pdf), PDF pages 3–7 and 33–34.

**Verified context:** The east riverbank historically contained marshes, creeks, and sloughs. Produce distribution, river shipping, rail connections, and later trucking shaped the district. The report describes older produce buildings between Water Avenue and Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard adapting to smaller manufacturing, industrial service, and office users.

**Design interpretation:** Use repeated structural bays, clear loading logic, and a compact street wall. Reserve a small, genuinely occupiable frontage for building operations, an interpretive room, or a workshop program; avoid a full blank security wall. An inset planting court can recall water’s presence without pretending to reconstruct a historic wetland.

**Limit:** The report’s business counts and tenancy descriptions are historical, not 2026 facts. The former wetland context does not establish current soil contamination, infiltration capacity, or flood designation for an assumed parcel.

### S2 — Material and reuse precedent

[City of Portland — Seismic retrofit of the Plow Works Building](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/news/2020/5/28/plans-examiner-john-cooley-recognized-his-work-seismically-retrofitted-111-year), 28 May 2020.

**Verified context:** The Central Eastside’s 1908 Plow Works Building is a four-story masonry former farm-equipment building. Its retrofit used a new structural system, and the article documents exposed wooden posts with steel connectors.

**Design interpretation:** Brick-red masonry, dark metal screens, and timber at the sheltered entry make a defensible local palette. Express an honest contemporary frame and deep wall openings rather than copying historic decoration. Keep the broad data-hall enclosure legible through a fine-grained bay rhythm.

**Limit:** This is a neighborhood precedent, not the assumed project site. Neither reclaimed material availability nor structural adequacy is established. “Reclaimed brick,” “mass timber,” and “low-carbon concrete” must remain proposed specifications until sourced and engineered.

### S3 — Freight and public movement

[PBOT — Central Eastside Freight Compatibility Study](https://www.portland.gov/transportation/pbot-projects/ccim/documents/ccim-central-eastside-freight-study/download), November 2018, PDF pages 2 and 15–16.

**Verified context:** The study identifies freight movement as critical to the district and says its streets are designated freight district streets. It describes loading zones and docks distributed throughout the area and notes that well-designed pedestrian, cycling, and transit changes can make movements more predictable.

**Design interpretation:** Draw a continuous pedestrian route, visible crossings, distinct visitor and service entries, and a contained loading court. Place seating and planting clear of swept truck paths. Treat the building’s curb frontage as working infrastructure and an everyday pedestrian environment.

**Limit:** This 2018 study is context, not a current street survey. Truck type, turn radii, curb rules, loading demand, and rights-of-way must be verified. Do not claim that a new curb extension or street closure is approved.

### S4 — Seasonal climate and stormwater landscape

[City of Portland — About Green Streets](https://www.portland.gov/bes/stormwater/about-green-streets).

**Verified context:** Portland uses planted areas between street and sidewalk to slow and filter runoff before it reaches the sewer. Plant selection responds to wet winter soil and dry summer conditions; rushes and sedges are common. Utilities, planting-strip width, trees, and parking influence siting.

**Design interpretation:** Show a rain canopy, robust drainage details, and planted stormwater edges with clear inlets and an overflow route. The landscape should look seasonally plausible: lush after winter rains, quieter and drier in summer. Use layered planting rather than a permanently saturated water garden.

**Limit:** The city’s general benefits are not project performance results. Label bioswales and roof runoff reuse as concepts requiring geotechnical, drainage, utility, and maintenance design. No infiltration volume, pollutant-removal percentage, or irrigation saving has been calculated. A commercial project must use the applicable stormwater approval process.

### S5 — Native palette

[City of Portland — Portland Plant List](https://www.portland.gov/bps/documents/portland-plant-list/download), June 2016 edition currently linked by the city; PDF pages 65, 75, 138, and 142.

**Verified context / proposed use:**

| Species | Verified characteristics | Proposed location |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red-flowering currant, *Ribes sanguineum* | Full to part sun; dry to moist soil; early flowers feed hummingbirds | Sunny upper planting edge |
| Vine maple, *Acer circinatum* | Native small tree; moist to seasonally wet soil; autumn color | Protected court with adequate soil volume |
| Sword fern, *Polystichum munitum* | Native evergreen fern; part sun/full shade listed | Shaded understory near entry |
| Slough sedge, *Carex obnupta* | Listed in local riparian and wetland communities | Wetter basin zone, subject to hydraulic design |

**Design interpretation:** A four-species framework can communicate local ecology without making the planting read as generic ornamental landscaping.

**Limit:** This is a concept palette, not an approved planting schedule. Match soil, exposure, ponding, mature dimensions, utilities, visibility, and maintenance needs. Native status does not mean every species belongs in every bioswale.

### S6 — River, ecology, and connected public space

[Portland Parks & Recreation — Eastbank Esplanade](https://www.portland.gov/parks/eastbank-esplanade).

**Verified context:** The 1.5-mile Esplanade connects the Hawthorne Bridge to the Steel Bridge and links to eastside neighborhoods. The city describes shallow habitat, native riverbank planting, treatment of some I-5 runoff, and fish-habitat work. It identifies salmon and steelhead among wildlife using this river environment.

**Design interpretation:** Orient the public entrance and wayfinding toward neighborhood-to-river movement. Make runoff treatment visible, with a small interpretation panel explaining the watershed connection. Use habitat planting at the upland street edge; keep drainage and planting maintainable.

**Limit:** The site is not established as riverfront or as an extension of the Esplanade. A planted courtyard does not demonstrate restored salmon habitat. No in-water construction, river intake, discharge, or new trail connection should be implied.

### S7 — Flood uncertainty

[City of Portland — Flood Hazard Regulations and Flood Maps](https://www.portland.gov/ppd/site-development-permits/floodplain-maps).

**Verified context:** Portland provides both FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Maps and the Composite February 1996 Flood Inundation Area mapping. Its guidance directs property searches to check the FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area and the 1996 inundation area.

**Design interpretation:** Keep critical electrical and IT equipment above a future verified flood design elevation; use a serviceable ground-level perimeter and a clear safe access strategy. The section may show an elevated equipment zone only as a precautionary concept.

**Limit:** Flood status and elevation for the illustrative boundary are unverified. Do not call the plot “outside the floodplain,” “flood-proof,” or “safe to build.” No numerical plinth height or design flood elevation has been determined.

### S8 — Noise as a design driver

[City of Portland — Code 18.10.010, Land Use Zones](https://www.portland.gov/code/18/10/010).

**Verified context:** Permissible sound is assessed at the receiving property line and varies with source and receiver zoning. The code provides cumulative adjustments for nighttime and narrow-band or steady sound and allows octave-band assessment in specified circumstances.

**Design interpretation:** Place fans and cooling equipment in a screened internal or rooftop zone set back from occupied edges. Use acoustic louvers, attenuated intakes, equipment isolation, and enclosure mass as proposed measures. Keep the public court away from direct fan discharge and routine generator testing.

**Limit:** Visual screens and vegetation are not proof of acoustic performance. Source/receiver zoning, sound power, tonality, operating schedule, nighttime conditions, and cumulative effects require an acoustic study. Do not publish a blanket dBA limit, predicted reduction, or compliance claim.

### S9 — Energy and cooling water

[U.S. Department of Energy, FEMP — Cooling Water Efficiency Opportunities for Federal Data Centers](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/cooling-water-efficiency-opportunities-federal-data-centers).

**Verified context:** Cooling-tower consumption is connected to IT heat load and heat-removal efficiency. Evaporation and blowdown both use water. FEMP defines site Water Usage Effectiveness as annual site water divided by annual IT energy, in liters per kWh, and describes opportunities to reduce cooling demand and water use.

**Design interpretation:** Develop cooling, energy, and water budgets together. Explore dry/hybrid heat rejection, appropriate operating temperatures, metering, and potential heat recovery as options. On-site solar can be drawn as an illustrative component; its contribution needs calculation. Publish measured annual and peak resource use if the project ever advances.

**Limit:** No IT load, cooling plant, utility interconnection, water availability, PUE, WUE, renewable supply, heat customer, or carbon result is established. A closed cooling loop does not by itself prove zero water consumption. Do not claim that rainwater or rooftop solar supplies the whole facility.

## Visual direction — all design interpretation

- **Massing:** compact warehouse-scale volumes; repeated bays; deep openings; a legible entry cut through the heavier envelope.
- **Material story:** mineral red masonry; graphite metal; warm timber at the human-scale entrance; pale concrete paving. These are proposed selections, not sampled site colors or carbon claims.
- **Landscape:** low wet-zone planting, taller currant groups on drier ground, shade ferns and vine maple in a protected court. Preserve visibility at crossings.
- **Public edge:** sheltered seating, cycle parking, understandable wayfinding, a small window into the facility’s purpose; secure boundaries integrated behind the public frontage.
- **Machine edge:** a contained service court and ventilated equipment screen; distinguish screening for appearance from tested sound attenuation.
- **Atmosphere:** soft overcast light and wet pavement are locally plausible imagery, but pair them with a warm-season view to avoid suggesting year-round rain.

Useful project labels: **Verified context** / **Design response** / **To verify**. A visible “Study assumptions” panel should include parcel, zoning, survey, flood, soils, electrical service, cooling/water demand, noise, security, and freight movement.
